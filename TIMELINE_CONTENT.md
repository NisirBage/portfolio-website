## Childhood

Long before any of this had a name, there were two things I couldn't put down: books and a pencil. I read voraciously, anything I could get my hands on, and spent just as many hours sketching and doodling. Somewhere in the middle of that was Pokémon, specifically Emerald and Platinum, and the pixelated, top-down world they were built in. I adored that aesthetic before I understood why. I still do. You're standing in it right now.

Consuming that much art, in that many forms, does something to you eventually. It doesn't stay passive. Somewhere along the way, the appetite for taking things in turned into an appetite for making things.

## School

The first thing I actually tried to build was a CNC plotter machine, for my 10th-grade IT Exhibition project. My team didn't win. One of the axes never worked quite right, and after weeks of effort, that stung. But it wasn't a defeat so much as a reality check. It was my first real deep dive into GitHub, documentation, and the unglamorous mechanics of actually building something, and it left me with a lasting respect for the technical depth hiding inside everyday devices nobody stops to think about.

I picked building back up in 11th grade with web development. Then senior secondary happened, the exam-cramming years, and serious projects went quiet for a while. They didn't come back until college.

## BIT Mesra

I didn't arrive at BIT Mesra chasing a tech career. I arrived chasing debate and literature. My school never had a real competition season, and I'd always felt that gap. College's competition-dense ecosystem gave me the chance to actually test myself against it, again and again, in a way I'd been missing. I threw myself into it: competed widely, represented the college at reputed forums, and eventually got good enough that schools and colleges started inviting me to chair and judge Model UNs.

Tech found its way back in sideways, through my peers. I noticed the people around me building web projects and started building alongside them. My AIML degree did the rest. It pointed me toward machine learning specifically, and it was my professors who pushed me to stop treating the theory as something that lived only in a classroom and start applying it to real problems.

## Machine Learning

The first project that felt genuinely mine, not just an assignment I turned in, was a gene-expression clustering system: fuzzy c-means, built on the Gene Expression Omnibus dataset, trying to classify the likelihood of AML versus ALL leukemia. It started as a course project for Unsupervised Learning. It's also where machine learning stopped being coursework and started being something I wanted to keep doing.

## Research

That interest carried into a summer research internship under Dr. Supreeti Kamilya, working on a very different kind of problem: detecting urea while cutting out interference from uric acid, without losing sensitivity to creatinine, a selectivity problem closer to analytical chemistry and instrumentation than anything I'd done before. It's still ongoing. I don't have a tidy ending for this one yet, and I'd rather say that honestly than pretend otherwise.

Between the leukemia clustering and the biosensor work, the pattern became hard to miss: whatever the specific problem, AI/ML and data analytics kept turning out to be the common thread underneath it.

## Leadership

Alongside all of this, I've been building a parallel track in leadership: Joint Secretary of UNESQUO, BIT Mesra's speaking and quizzing organization, and Deputy Editor-in-Chief of the Literary Society, both for 2025-26. This year, my final year, I've stepped up to Editor-in-Chief of the Literary Society. It's less one defining moment than a pattern I can trace back through years of leading teams: competitions, course projects, whatever needed someone to hold it together, until leading eventually just became something I do, not something that happened to me once.

## Current Work

Right now I'm still working through that research internship, and actively looking toward what's next: data analytics, ML-adjacent roles, consulting. Nothing's settled yet, and that's deliberate.

## What's Next

If there's a single thing this whole path has taught me, it's this: get comfortable with disappointment and embarrassment, because that's the only healthy way through failure. The CNC plotter taught me that first, and I've relearned it since. And always go looking for a reference, a document, someone else's perspective, before assuming your own first instinct is the whole picture. Everything after the plotter (the debate rounds, the leukemia clustering, the biosensor work, the leadership roles) has been some version of that same lesson, applied again.
